Does water block UVA? Swimming, snorkelling and sunscreen

Many people treat the water as shade: a swim feels like a break from the sun. It isn't. Water is one of the weakest UV shields you will meet, and it is weakest of all against UVA. Add the surface reflection, the cooling that hides a burn, and the way swimming washes sunscreen off, and the beach, the pool and the boat end up among the highest-exposure settings there are. This article explains how far UV gets into water, why the answer depends so much on which water, and what actually protects you.

The one-line version: half a metre of clear water still leaves you with most of the UVA above it. In a pool or a clear sea, you are not in the shade — you are in the sun, wet, with less sunscreen on than you think.

Why water lets UVA through

Pure water is remarkably transparent to ultraviolet. It absorbs strongly in the infrared — which is why water feels like it is keeping you cool — but its absorption in the UVA band is tiny. That is why the colour of the water, not the water itself, decides how much UV reaches you below the surface.

What absorbs UV in natural water is mostly dissolved organic matter — the tannins and humic substances that tint lakes, rivers and coastal water yellow-brown — plus suspended sediment and plankton. Those absorb short wavelengths much more strongly than long ones, so UVB is removed first and UVA penetrates further. It is the same asymmetry seen in glass and in cloud: filters that remove the band that burns you while passing much of the band that ages you.

These are indicative ranges; real values depend on the sun angle, the waves, and the exact water.[1] The most commonly quoted public-health figure is that at half a metre below the surface, UV is still around 40% of the surface value — and that is for sunburning UV.[2] Because UVA is absorbed less, the share of UVA still present at that depth is higher.

The crucial point is that the protective cases — dark lakes, depth — are not where swimmers spend their time. The skin that is underwater during a swim is usually within 10–30 cm of the surface, where even murky water has done very little.

Above the water: the surface adds to the problem

A calm water surface reflects only a few percent of UV from a high sun, rising sharply as the sun gets lower and the angle becomes glancing. That is far less than snow, but on a boat or a beach it adds to the direct sun and the large diffuse sky component. The reflection article covers the numbers, including why boaters burn under the chin and nose.

Why swimmers burn without noticing

  • Water cools the skin. The heat that normally warns you of strong sun is removed, so a burn develops silently and shows up hours later.
  • The exposed areas are unusual. Snorkellers present their back, shoulders and the backs of their legs to the sun for an hour at a time — areas that rarely get sun otherwise and are often missed with sunscreen.
  • Wet fabric protects less. A wet cotton T-shirt can lose a large part of its protection. Purpose-made swimwear and rash vests hold up much better; see does clothing block UVA.
  • Sunscreen washes and rubs off. Even water-resistant products lose protection with swimming, and towelling removes much of what is left.

What "water resistant" actually means

No sunscreen is waterproof, and regulators in most major markets no longer allow the word. What the label does guarantee is the result of a standardised immersion test:[3]

LabelRegionWhat was tested
Water resistant (40 minutes)USLabelled SPF retained after 40 minutes in water
Water resistant (80 minutes)USLabelled SPF retained after 80 minutes in water
Water resistantEU / UKAt least 50% of SPF retained after 2 × 20 minutes
Very water resistantEU / UKAt least 50% of SPF retained after 4 × 20 minutes

Two caveats follow from that. First, the tests measure SPF, which is essentially a UVB number; there is no separate UVA water-resistance claim, and UVA protection depends on how evenly the remaining film covers the skin. Second, the tests do not include towelling, sand or rubbing against a board or wetsuit, all of which remove sunscreen mechanically. Reapply after every swim and every towel-off, and at the latest when the labelled time runs out.

Protection that works in and around water

  • Cover rather than coat. A long-sleeved rash vest or swim shirt and, for snorkelling, swim leggings remove the problem areas entirely and do not wash off.
  • Use a water-resistant sunscreen on what is left — face, ears, neck, hands and the backs of the knees — and apply it generously 15–20 minutes before getting in so the film can set. See how much to apply.
  • Reapply after drying off, not just on a clock.
  • Wear UV400 sunglasses on boats and beaches, where reflected and scattered light reach the eyes from below and the side. See UVA and your eyes.
  • Plan around the peak. UVA stays high for longer around midday than the UV Index suggests; here is the daily curve.

The UVA Index on this site estimates UVA on an open horizontal surface on land; it does not model water depth or the water surface itself. As a rule of thumb, treat time near the surface of a pool or clear sea as roughly the same exposure as the open beach — and time on the beach as somewhat more than the number, once sand and water reflection are added.
