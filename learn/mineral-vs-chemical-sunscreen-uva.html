Mineral vs chemical sunscreen: which is better for UVA?

It is one of the most-asked sunscreen questions, and the honest answer is that the category on the front of the bottle tells you less than the ingredient list on the back. Zinc oxide is an excellent UVA filter; titanium dioxide on its own is not. Some organic filters are among the best UVA protectors made; others barely touch UVA at all. This article sorts out which is which, clears up the persistent "mineral reflects, chemical absorbs" myth, and covers the practical trade-offs — cast, skin sensitivity, reef rules and absorption — that usually decide the choice.

The one-line version: for UVA, choose by filter and by tested UVA rating, not by the word "mineral" or "chemical". A zinc-oxide mineral product and a well-formulated modern organic one can both give excellent UVA protection; a titanium-dioxide-only product or an unstabilised avobenzone formula may not.

The two families, in plain terms

Mineral (also called physical or inorganic) sunscreens use one or both of two metal oxides: zinc oxide and titanium dioxide. They are insoluble particles suspended in the cream.

Chemical (better called organic) sunscreens use carbon-based molecules dissolved in the formula — avobenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, bemotrizinol and many others. The labels are slightly misleading in both directions: zinc oxide is just as much a chemical as avobenzone, and a few "organic" filters such as bisoctrizole (Tinosorb M) are actually particles that behave partly like a mineral.

The "mineral reflects UV" myth

The usual story is that mineral sunscreens sit on the skin like tiny mirrors bouncing UV away, while chemical ones soak it up and convert it to heat. The second half is right. The first half mostly is not.

When the optical properties of the zinc oxide and titanium dioxide particles used in modern sunscreens were measured directly, they turned out to protect overwhelmingly by absorption — the particles are semiconductors that absorb photons above their band-gap energy — with only a small share, on the order of a few percent, coming from reflection and scattering.[1] The large, chalky particles of old-style zinc paste scattered more, which is where the white cast and the mirror story came from; the micronised and nano grades in today's products trade most of that scattering for transparency.

This matters for one practical reason: it means mineral sunscreens are not inherently "safer" or "gentler" because they deflect UV rather than absorb it. Both families absorb. The genuine differences lie elsewhere.

UVA coverage, filter by filter

UVA runs from 315 to 400 nm, and its long-wave half — UVA-I, 340–400 nm — is the hardest part to cover. That is where filters separate.

Read the table this way: a mineral sunscreen built on zinc oxide covers UVA well; a mineral sunscreen built only on titanium dioxide can carry a high SPF while leaving the long UVA band thin. On the organic side, a formula built from octinoxate and homosalate is close to a UVB-only product, while one containing bemotrizinol, bisoctrizole or DHHB is among the best UVA protection available. Availability of these filters differs sharply by country — the UVA label guide covers which are approved where.

Photostability: the avobenzone problem

A filter that breaks down in sunlight loses protection while you are wearing it. Avobenzone, the most widely used UVA-I filter in the US, is the classic case: on its own it can lose a large share of its absorbance within an hour of sun. Formulators fix this by pairing it with stabilisers — octocrylene, bemotrizinol and several others — and a well-made avobenzone product holds up fine. A poorly made one does not, and you cannot tell from the label alone.

Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide do not photodegrade in that way. That is a real point in mineral filters' favour, and part of the reason zinc oxide is a common recommendation for anyone who cannot easily check how a product was tested.

The white-cast row deserves a note. It is the main reason people give up on mineral sunscreen, and it falls hardest on darker skin tones. Tinted mineral products solve it, and the iron oxides that do the tinting add something no clear sunscreen offers: protection against visible light, which matters for melasma and hyperpigmentation.

Safety questions people ask

Absorption into the body. Studies run under the US FDA found that several organic filters, including oxybenzone, avobenzone and octocrylene, are absorbed into the bloodstream above the threshold that triggers further safety testing. That threshold is a regulatory trigger for more data, not evidence of harm, and the regulators and dermatology bodies that reviewed it continue to recommend sunscreen use. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are the two filters the FDA has classed as generally recognised as safe and effective.

Nanoparticles. The nano zinc oxide and titanium dioxide in transparent mineral sunscreens have repeatedly been found not to penetrate meaningfully beyond the outermost dead layer of intact skin. Sprays and powders are a separate question because of inhalation, which is one reason to prefer lotions.

Reefs. Hawaii, Key West, Palau and some other jurisdictions restrict oxybenzone and octinoxate because of laboratory evidence of harm to corals. The real-world contribution of sunscreen to reef decline, compared with warming water, is debated. If you are swimming in a place with a ban, follow it; the simplest compliant choice is a zinc oxide product.

How to choose

  • Want the simplest reliable UVA option? A zinc oxide product, ideally tinted, with a tested UVA rating. See how to read PA, the UVA circle and star ratings.
  • Hate the cast or the texture? A modern organic formula with bemotrizinol, bisoctrizole, DHHB or stabilised avobenzone. In practice, the sunscreen you will actually wear generously beats the theoretically superior one you won't.
  • Sensitive skin, eczema, or a small child? Mineral first, fragrance-free. See sun protection for children and babies.
  • Prone to pigmentation? A tinted product with iron oxides, mineral or hybrid.
  • Whatever you pick, use enough. UVA protection falls faster than SPF when you under-apply — this is the step most people get wrong.

And match the effort to the day. The UVA Index shows when long-wave UV is high enough that full protection is worth it, including the overcast and winter days the ordinary UV Index tends to understate.
