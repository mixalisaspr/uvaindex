The dangers of UVA radiation

UVA rarely gives you the obvious warning of a sunburn, which is exactly what makes it easy to underestimate. Its harm is mostly cumulative and delayed — built up quietly over years of everyday exposure, including time spent indoors near windows or in the car.

The core problem: UVA penetrates deep into the skin, there's a lot of it (~95% of surface UV), it passes through glass and cloud, and it doesn't burn — so the body's "you've had enough sun" alarm never goes off.

1. Photoaging

This is UVA's signature effect. By reaching the dermis, UVA degrades collagen and elastin — the proteins that keep skin firm and springy — and disrupts their repair. Over time that shows up as wrinkles, sagging, a leathery texture, broken capillaries and uneven pigmentation (sun spots). Researchers estimate the large majority of visible skin aging is caused by sun exposure rather than the passage of years — one widely cited study of facial ageing put the figure near 80% — and UVA is the principal culprit.[1][2]

2. Indirect DNA damage

Where UVB tends to damage DNA directly, UVA acts mostly indirectly: it's absorbed by other molecules in the skin and generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) — unstable, reactive molecules that go on to damage DNA, proteins and cell membranes through oxidative stress. This roundabout route still produces mutations, and it's a key mechanism behind UVA's role in skin cancer.[3] UVA is not purely indirect, though: in whole human skin it also produces the same cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers that UVB does, just far less efficiently per photon.[4]

3. Skin cancer, including melanoma

Ultraviolet radiation as a whole is classified as a known human carcinogen.[5] UVB has long been tied to common non-melanoma skin cancers, but evidence increasingly implicates UVA in melanoma, the most dangerous form, as well as in overall photocarcinogenesis.[6] Because both bands contribute, sun protection has to cover both — the reason dermatologists stress broad-spectrum products, not just a high SPF (which mainly reflects UVB protection).

4. Immune suppression

UVA (and UV more broadly) can locally suppress the skin's immune response. That's thought to reduce the body's ability to detect and clear newly mutated or pre-cancerous cells — another pathway by which chronic exposure raises long-term risk.[3]

5. Eyes

The eyes are vulnerable too. Cumulative ultraviolet exposure is associated with cataracts — the strongest evidence is for UVB, with UVA a plausible contributor — and with other eye conditions.[7][8] UV-blocking sunglasses — ones rated to block UVA and UVB, ideally wrap-around — meaningfully reduce the dose reaching the eye. UVA and your eyes covers the standards, why frame shape beats lens darkness, and why children need more protection than adults.

6. Photosensitivity and medication reactions

UVA drives many photosensitivity reactions: rashes or exaggerated "sunburn-like" responses triggered by certain medications (some antibiotics, diuretics and others), cosmetics, or conditions like polymorphous light eruption.[9] Because UVA passes through glass, sensitive individuals can react even indoors — see photosensitivity and UVA for the drug classes and the protection that actually works.

How to reduce UVA exposure

  • Use broad-spectrum sunscreen. Look for "broad spectrum" and UVA indicators — the circled UVA logo or a high PA+++/PA++++ rating — not just a high SPF number. Decoding the UVA labels explains what each mark guarantees.
  • Don't trust glass. Windows block UVB but not most UVA; UV-protective film helps for cars and homes if you spend long periods by a window.
  • Cover up. Tightly woven clothing, wide-brimmed hats and UV-rated fabrics (UPF) are reliable because they don't wear off.
  • Protect your eyes. Sunglasses rated UV400, for UVA and UVB.
  • Remember it's there on cloudy days and outside summer — UVA falls far less than UVB does.
  • Skip tanning beds. Many emit high, concentrated UVA — as do gel-manicure lamps.
  • Mind reflective ground. Snow, sand and water bounce UVA up onto skin that direct sun never reaches.
Know your dose: Because UVA isn't reflected well by the standard UV Index, it helps to look at UVA directly. This site's UVA Index estimates surface UVA irradiance for your location and time so you can see when it's actually high.
