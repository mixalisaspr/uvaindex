Sun protection for babies and children

Children are not small adults when it comes to ultraviolet. Their skin is thinner, their eyes let more UV through to the retina, they spend more time outdoors at the worst hours, and they cannot judge or report a burn until it has happened. Sunburn in childhood is one of the best established risk factors for melanoma later in life — and the long-wave UVA that drives cumulative damage reaches them through cloud, glass and the water just as it does adults. This guide covers what is different about children, what age sunscreen becomes appropriate, and the habits that do most of the protecting.

The one-line version: shade, clothing, a hat and sunglasses first; sunscreen on what is left. Under six months, keep babies out of direct sun altogether.

Why children are more vulnerable

  • Thinner skin, less established pigmentation. The outer layer of a young child's skin is thinner and their melanin response is less developed, so a given dose of UV does more.
  • Clearer eyes. The lens of the eye yellows with age and gradually absorbs more UVA and blue light. A child's lens is much clearer, so a larger share of UVA reaches the back of the eye. See UVA and your eyes.
  • More time outdoors, at midday. School break times, sports and summer holidays put children outside around solar noon, when UV peaks.
  • They do not notice in time. A child absorbed in play will not take themselves into the shade, and sunburn shows up hours after the damage is done.

What the evidence says about childhood sunburn

Large studies have consistently found that blistering sunburns in childhood and adolescence are associated with a substantially higher risk of melanoma in adulthood. A comprehensive meta-analysis found that sunburn raises melanoma risk at every age, childhood included — so the aim is to avoid burns throughout life, starting early.[1] The same research tradition is also why Australia, which has the world's highest skin cancer rates, built its schools around "no hat, no play" policies and shaded playgrounds.

It is worth correcting one widely repeated figure: the claim that "80% of your lifetime sun exposure happens before 18". When researchers actually measured exposure, they found people receive roughly a quarter of their lifetime dose by 18, with exposure continuing steadily through adult life.[2] That does not make childhood protection less important — burns and habits in childhood matter disproportionately — but it does mean adult sun protection is far from a lost cause.

Babies under six months

Most guidance agrees on the principle: keep young babies out of direct sunlight. Their skin is especially thin, and there is little safety data on sunscreen use at this age. In practice:

  • Use shade — a canopy, parasol or tree — and move it as the sun moves.
  • Dress them in light, long, tightly woven clothing and a wide-brimmed hat or bonnet with a neck flap.
  • Avoid draping a blanket over a pram: it traps heat dangerously and does not stop the sky-scattered UV from the sides.
  • If shade and clothing are not enough, the American Academy of Pediatrics says a small amount of sunscreen on small areas such as the face and the backs of the hands is acceptable.[3] National guidance varies, so check yours.

Test a new product on a small patch of the child's arm first. Apply it 15–20 minutes before going out, generously — an adult-sized dose for the face and neck is roughly a teaspoon, and a small child needs proportionally the same density — and reapply every two hours and after swimming or towelling. The application guide explains why "a thin layer" gives a fraction of the labelled protection.

Clothing, hats and sunglasses do most of the work

Sunscreen is the last line of defence, not the first. For children, physical cover has big advantages: it does not wear off, cannot be missed in patches, and does not depend on a negotiation every two hours.

  • Clothing: long sleeves in tightly woven or UPF-rated fabric, and a rash vest for the beach and pool. See does clothing block UVA.
  • Hats: broad-brimmed or legionnaire-style with a neck flap. Baseball caps leave the ears, cheeks and neck exposed.
  • Sunglasses: close-fitting, wraparound, labelled UV400 or meeting a recognised standard. Toy sunglasses with dark but non-UV-blocking lenses can be worse than none, because dark lenses dilate the pupil.
  • Shade: real shade blocks a lot of the sky, not just the sun. A small umbrella on a bright beach leaves a large share of UV coming in from the sky and the sand; see reflected UVA.

Timing and everyday habits

The most effective habit is simply knowing when UV is high. Sun is strongest around solar noon, which in summer time is often closer to 13:00 than 12:00, and UVA stays substantial for longer either side of it than the sunburn-weighted UV Index suggests. Cloudy days are not safe days — much of the UVA gets through cloud — and children in car seats by a side window receive UVA through glass that will never burn them.

  • Plan outdoor play for the morning and late afternoon in summer.
  • Make hats and sunscreen part of the routine, like shoes, rather than a decision made each day.
  • Teach the shadow rule to older children: if your shadow is shorter than you, find shade.
  • Be the example. Children copy what adults do, not what they say.
