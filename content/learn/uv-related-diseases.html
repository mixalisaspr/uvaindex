UV-related diseases: which are driven by UVA and which by UVB

Ultraviolet light is linked to a long list of diseases. It causes some of them, such as skin cancer, it triggers others, such as sun rashes, and it worsens others, such as lupus. The band involved is not always the same. Some conditions are driven by UVB, the sunburning band the UV Index tracks, and several of the most troublesome are driven mainly by UVA.

Why the band matters: UVA passes through window glass, falls far less than UVB under cloud, and stays relatively high early, late and in winter. A condition driven by UVA can flare indoors, in the car or on a day when the UV Index reads low. A condition driven by UVB mostly cannot.

Quick reference

"Main band" is the part of the spectrum most responsible. Few conditions are purely one band, and individual patients vary, especially in the photodermatoses.

Diseases UV causes

  • Basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma. The most common cancers in fair-skinned populations, together with their precursor, actinic keratosis. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies solar UV radiation as carcinogenic to humans across the whole spectrum.[[cite:iarc100d]] UVB does most of the direct DNA damage, but UVA also produces the same signature lesions in human skin.[[cite:mouret2006]]
  • Melanoma. Both bands are involved. In animal models, UVA-induced melanoma depends on melanin pigment, pointing to an indirect, oxidative route that UVB does not need.[[cite:noonan2012]] Tanning beds, which emit mostly UVA, measurably raise melanoma risk.[[cite:boniol2012]] See the dangers of UVA and UVA indoors.
  • Photoaging. Wrinkles, loss of elasticity and uneven pigment are mainly a UVA effect, because UVA reaches the dermis where collagen sits.[[cite:fisher2002]] See UVA and skin aging.
  • Immune suppression. Both bands dampen the skin's immune response, which helps sun-damaged cells escape detection.[[cite:halliday2011]] It is not a disease in itself, but it links UV exposure to cancer.
  • Eye disease. Photokeratitis, a sunburn of the cornea, is a UVB effect. Cortical cataract has been linked to cumulative UVB exposure,[[cite:taylor1988]] and pterygium to chronic UV exposure concentrated by the eye's own optics.[[cite:coroneo2011]] See UVA and your eyes.

Diseases UV triggers

The photodermatoses are conditions in which normal amounts of sunlight provoke an abnormal reaction.[[cite:lehmann2011]] This is where UVA matters most: many of them are triggered mainly or partly by UVA, so UVB-weighted advice and high-SPF sunscreen with weak UVA cover can fail.

  • Polymorphous light eruption (PMLE). The most common photodermatosis: an itchy rash that appears hours to days after the first strong sun of spring or a holiday. It is mostly UVA-triggered, which is why it can appear despite a high-SPF sunscreen.[[cite:lehmann2011]]
  • Actinic prurigo and hydroa vacciniforme. Rarer photodermatoses that usually start in childhood. Both are provoked mainly by UVA.[[cite:lehmann2011]]
  • Solar urticaria. Hives within minutes of exposure. The triggering wavelengths differ from patient to patient and often include UVA and visible light, so phototesting is useful.[[cite:lehmann2011]]
  • Chronic actinic dermatitis. Persistent eczema on sun-exposed skin, usually in older men, with sensitivity that is typically broad, across UVB, UVA and often visible light.[[cite:lehmann2011]]
  • The porphyrias. In erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP), porphyrins that build up in the skin absorb light and cause burning pain within minutes, often with little to see.[[cite:lecha2009]] The absorption peak, the Soret band, sits around 400 nm, at the border between long UVA and violet visible light. Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT) involves the same kind of light absorption but shows as fragile, blistering skin on the backs of the hands. Ordinary sunscreens do little against visible light; tinted, iron-oxide formulas and window film rated beyond 400 nm help more.
  • Drug-induced photosensitivity. Most photosensitising drugs absorb in the UVA range.[[cite:blakely2019]] Common examples are doxycycline, fluoroquinolones, hydrochlorothiazide, amiodarone and some NSAIDs. Photosensitivity and UVA lists the drug classes and the protection that works.
  • Cold sores. UV exposure of the lips can reactivate the herpes simplex virus; recurrences have been provoked experimentally with a sunlamp.[[cite:spruance1985]] The effect is generally attributed to the sunburning range, so this is mainly a UVB trigger.

Diseases UV makes worse

  • Lupus erythematosus. Photosensitivity is a core feature. Phototesting of hundreds of patients has reproduced skin lesions with UVA as well as UVB,[[cite:kuhn2001]] and exposure can flare systemic disease as well as the skin. This is why year-round, broad-spectrum protection, including behind glass, is standard advice.
  • Melasma and other hyperpigmentation. Driven by long-wavelength UVA and visible light rather than UVB,[[cite:passeron2018,mahmoud2010]] which is why tinted sunscreens outperform clear ones. See UVA, melasma and skin tone.
  • Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP). An inherited failure to repair UV-damaged DNA, with a skin cancer risk thousands of times higher than normal in young patients.[[cite:digiovanna2012]] UVB causes most of the damage XP cells cannot repair, but UVA causes some of the same lesions,[[cite:mouret2006]] so protection has to cover all UV, indoors included. XP is a UVB-first disease, not a UVA one.

The other side: UV that helps

The same bands are also useful. UVB is what makes vitamin D in the skin, which is why synthesis stops in high-latitude winters when UVB is scarce;[[cite:webb1988]] UVA does almost nothing for it. Dermatologists also use controlled UV as treatment: narrowband UVB for psoriasis, eczema and vitiligo, PUVA (a UVA-activated psoralen) for stubborn psoriasis, and UVA1 for some forms of eczema and scleroderma. These treatments are dosed and supervised, which is the opposite of casual sun exposure.

What this means for protection

If your condition is in the UVA-driven group:

  1. Choose sunscreen for its UVA rating, not its SPF. Look for the circled UVA mark, PA++++ or a high UVA star rating. Decoding the labels explains each mark.
  2. Do not trust glass. Car side windows and ordinary glazing let most UVA through; clear UV film fixes that. See does glass block UVA.
  3. Add visible-light protection for porphyria, solar urticaria triggered by visible light, or melasma: tinted iron-oxide sunscreens or opaque cover.
  4. Watch UVA, not just the UV Index. The UVA Index shows when UVA is high even if the standard index is low, for example on overcast days and in winter.

This article is an overview, not a diagnosis. If you react to sunlight in ways you cannot explain, a dermatologist can run phototests to find out which wavelengths trigger you, and that tells you which band to protect against.
