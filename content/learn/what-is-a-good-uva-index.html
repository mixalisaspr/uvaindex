What is a good UVA Index? Reading the scale

The calculator returns a single number between 0 and about 11. This page is about what that number means — what changes between one band and the next, why the boundaries sit where they do, and what a given reading should actually change about your day. If you want the physics of how the number is produced, that lives in how the UVA Index is calculated.

The short answer: under 3 is Low and needs no thought. 3–6 is Moderate. 6 and above is where UVA accumulates fast enough that daily protection is worth the trouble. There is no level at which UVA does nothing — only levels at which it acts slowly.

The scale, band by band

The UVA Index is surface UVA irradiance in W/m² divided by six. That divisor is chosen so a clear-sky overhead sun — about 66 W/m² — lands on 11, giving the same top-of-scale feel as a peak tropical UV Index. Because the conversion is a single constant, every band boundary is exactly the index value multiplied by six.

Note that the scale is not capped at 11. Altitude alone can push it past the top: the model adds about 6% per kilometre, so a clear noon in Quito or La Paz reads comfortably above 12. Snow underfoot adds more. Eleven is a reference point, not a ceiling.

Why the bands say "Low" and not "safe"

The band names and colours deliberately reuse the WHO UV Index vocabulary — Low, Moderate, High, Very High, Extreme — because that vocabulary is already familiar and the numbers already sit on a comparable range. But the words carry a meaning for UV Index that they should not carry here, and the difference matters.

The UV Index bands are built around sunburn. They work because erythema has a threshold: below a certain dose your skin simply does not redden, so "Low" genuinely means "nothing will happen today". UVA's principal effects — collagen breakdown, pigmentation, lens and retinal exposure — have no comparable threshold. They are cumulative and largely silent. A "Low" UVA reading means the dose is arriving slowly, not that it has stopped.

These bands are unofficial. No standards body defines UVA categories. The WHO, WMO and ICNIRP all define the erythemal UV Index and stop there. The boundaries on this site are a pragmatic mapping chosen for familiarity, not a regulatory threshold, and nothing here is medical advice — see the About page.

What each band is worth doing something about

  • Low (under 3). No specific action for most people. The exception is anyone photosensitive — through medication, lupus, porphyria or PMLE — for whom the reaction threshold can sit well inside this band. See photosensitivity and UVA.
  • Moderate (3 to 6). Worth protecting for a deliberate session outdoors — a long walk, lunch outside, an afternoon in the garden. Not worth reorganising your day around. If you are treating melasma or hyperpigmentation, this is already a meaningful level; those conditions respond to UVA and visible light at doses far below anything that burns.
  • High (6 to 8). Broad-spectrum sunscreen on exposed skin if you will be out for more than a short while, and UV400 sunglasses. This is the band where a normal working day with any outdoor component starts contributing real cumulative dose.
  • Very High (8 to 11). Treat as a full-protection day: sunscreen applied properly and reapplied, covering clothing, a brimmed hat, shade during the middle hours. Reflective ground — snow, sand, water, pale concrete — adds to whatever the number says.
  • Extreme (11 and above). Everything above, and genuinely limit midday exposure. You will typically only see this at altitude, in the tropics, or over snow, and it is often accompanied by cool air that removes the usual instinct to seek shade.

When the UVA Index and the UV Index disagree

This is the reading that makes a separate UVA number worth having. The two indices are computed from different quantities, so they can and do diverge — and they diverge in exactly the situations where people relax.

The pattern is consistent: the UV Index and the UVA Index agree when the sun is high and the sky is clear, and separate whenever something — a low sun, cloud, glass, a season — removes UVB preferentially. Those are precisely the conditions people read as safe. The full argument is in what the UV Index measures, and the seasonal case is worked through in why UVA changes so little with the seasons.

How much to trust a given reading

The number is a model output, not a measurement. It is built from solar geometry — which is essentially exact — plus live cloud, aerosol and elevation data, which are not. That gives it a predictable error structure worth knowing:

  • Clear sky, sun well up: most reliable. Geometry dominates and the corrections are small. Treat the number as good to within a band.
  • Broken cloud: least reliable, and biased low. The model is attenuation-only, so it cannot produce the cloud enhancement effect, where sun in a gap between bright cumulus briefly delivers more than clear sky. Under those conditions reality can exceed the reading.
  • Sun near the horizon: least reliable in relative terms. Air mass grows quickly, small angular errors matter more, and the result is a small number anyway.
  • Reflective ground is only partly included. The albedo term is a modest enhancement for sand and snow. Standing in a snowfield surrounded by reflective slopes exceeds what a single surface multiplier can express — see reflected UVA.

Read the band rather than the decimal. The difference between 7.4 and 7.9 is inside the model's uncertainty; the difference between 4 and 9 is not.
