Does UVA damage hair? Colour, protein and your scalp

Hair is dead tissue, so it cannot burn — but it can certainly be damaged by sunlight, and the damage splits neatly along the UVA–UVB divide. UVB mainly attacks the protein of the hair shaft; UVA mainly bleaches its colour. That is why a summer outdoors leaves hair both lighter and rougher, why blonde, grey and dyed hair suffer most, and why the skin under the hair — the scalp — is one of the most neglected areas in sun protection.

The one-line version: summer lightening and colour fading are mostly UVA; dryness, frizz and breakage are mostly UVB, made worse by chlorine, salt and heat. A hat protects both your hair and the scalp beneath it far better than any product.

What sunlight does to a hair

A hair shaft has three layers: the overlapping scales of the cuticle on the outside, the cortex of keratin protein and melanin pigment that gives hair its strength and colour, and in thicker hairs a soft central medulla. Ultraviolet light reaches all of them, and different wavelengths do different things.

Visible light, particularly the blue end, contributes to colour fading as well, which is why even a product with UV filters only slows the process rather than stopping it.

Whose hair suffers most

  • Blonde and grey hair. Melanin, particularly the darker eumelanin, absorbs UV and soaks up the free radicals it creates, sacrificing itself to protect the protein. Hair with little melanin has less of that buffer, so its protein degrades faster.
  • Colour-treated hair. Dye molecules are more fragile than natural melanin, and bleaching has already opened the cuticle. Reds and vivid fashion shades fade fastest.
  • Long hair. The ends of long hair may be several years old and have accumulated every summer since they grew.
  • Swimmers. Chlorine and sun damage compound one another, and the familiar green tint in light hair comes from copper in pool water, not from chlorine itself. Salt water dries the cuticle and leaves crystals that roughen it further.

Your scalp: the forgotten skin

The scalp is skin like any other, and it is the most directly sun-facing skin on the body. Dense hair is a very effective shield, but three situations leave it exposed:

  • The parting. A straight parting can leave a strip of scalp almost fully exposed to a high sun. Sunburn there is common and easy to miss.
  • Thinning hair and baldness. As hair density falls, protection falls with it. The scalp is a well-known site for skin cancers — including some of the more dangerous melanomas — in men with thinning or no hair, and those cancers are often found late because no one can see them.
  • Children and short haircuts. Closely cropped hair and the crown of a young child's head get far more sun than adults assume.

Because UVA penetrates deeper into skin and drives cumulative damage without burning, a scalp that rarely reddens can still be accumulating it. See the dangers of UVA for the mechanisms.

Note that hair products with UV filters carry no standardised protection rating in any major market, so claims cannot be compared the way SPF or UPF can. They help, mainly against colour fading, but they are no substitute for a hat.

Practical summary

  • Wear a hat on high-UV days, especially if your hair is fair, grey, dyed or thinning.
  • Cover your parting with a scalp sunscreen or move it from day to day in summer.
  • Rinse after swimming, and wet hair with fresh water before getting into a pool so it absorbs less chlorinated water.
  • Use a UV-filter leave-in if colour fading is your concern, knowing it slows rather than stops it.
  • Ask someone to check your scalp, or mention it at skin checks — it is one of the places skin cancers are most often missed.
