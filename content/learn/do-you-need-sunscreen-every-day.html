Do you need sunscreen every day?

"Wear sunscreen every day, even when it's cloudy" is one of the most repeated pieces of skincare advice — and one of the most questioned. The useful answer is neither "always" nor "only at the beach". It depends on how much UVA actually reaches your skin, and UVA behaves differently from the sunburn-weighted UV Index most people check: it stays high through cloud, varies much less with season, and comes through windows. This guide goes situation by situation so you can decide for your own days.

The one-line version: if your normal day includes a commute, time outside or a seat by a window, daily sunscreen on your face, neck and hands is worth it. If you are indoors away from windows all day, it adds little.

Why "every day" advice exists

Sunburn is a UVB effect, and UVB really is low on overcast days, in winter and behind glass. If burning were the only concern, sunscreen on those days would be pointless. But skin aging, pigmentation and a share of the DNA damage behind skin cancer are driven largely by UVA, which does not warn you with a burn and accumulates quietly over years. "Every day" advice is really advice about cumulative UVA.

The best direct evidence comes from a randomised trial in Queensland, Australia, which assigned adults to apply sunscreen daily or at their own discretion. After four and a half years, the daily group showed no detectable increase in skin aging, while the discretionary group aged measurably.[[cite:hughes2013]] The same trial reported fewer melanomas over long-term follow-up in the daily-use group, a difference that fell just short of conventional statistical significance.[[cite:green2011]] Queensland is sunnier than most places, so the size of the benefit elsewhere is smaller — but the direction is clear.

Cloudy days

Cloud cuts UV far less than it cuts brightness. Thin or broken cloud often lets most UVA through, and the edges of bright cumulus can briefly increase UV above clear-sky levels.[[cite:calbo2005]] Only thick, dark overcast produces large reductions. The cloudy-day article has the numbers by cloud type.

Winter

The ozone layer strips out UVB much more when the sun is low, so in winter the UV Index can sit at 1 or 2 while UVA is still a substantial share of its summer value. That is why winter UV is mostly UVA. Whether that adds up to a meaningful dose depends on how long you are out: a few minutes on a dark northern-European day is very little, while a ski day is among the highest exposures of the year.

Indoors and in the car

This is where UVA differs most from the everyday intuition. Ordinary window glass blocks nearly all UVB — so you never burn indoors — but transmits a large share of UVA. Hours beside a sunny window, or a daily commute with the sun on the driver's-side window, add up to real exposure, concentrated on one side of the face. The glass article covers how much different windows let through and how UV film fixes it.

What does not matter is indoor lighting or screens. LED and fluorescent room lighting and phone, tablet and computer screens emit essentially no UV; see UVA indoors.

Skin tone, conditions and medication

  • Darker skin is much better protected against burning, and skin cancer is less common, but it is very prone to UVA- and visible-light-driven pigmentation and melasma. Daily use of a tinted sunscreen is often what dermatologists recommend for that reason; see UVA, melasma and skin tone.
  • Photosensitising medications and conditions are mostly triggered by UVA, which reaches you through cloud and glass. If you are on one, daily protection matters much more; see photosensitivity.
  • A history of skin cancer or many moles is a reason to follow your dermatologist's advice, which will usually be daily protection.

What "daily sunscreen" should look like

  • Face, neck, ears and backs of the hands — the skin that is exposed every day. Arms too if you drive a lot.
  • SPF 30 or more with a real UVA rating — PA+++ or higher, the circled UVA logo, or high UVA stars. See UVA labels decoded.
  • A product you like using. A moisturiser or tinted product with good UVA protection is easier to stick with than a beach sunscreen. Choose filters you tolerate; see mineral vs chemical sunscreen.
  • Enough of it. About a teaspoon for the face and neck. Makeup with SPF is applied far too thinly to deliver its labelled rating on its own; see how much sunscreen to apply.
  • Reapply on days you are outside for long periods; a single morning application is fine for an office day.

What about vitamin D?

Vitamin D is made by UVB, not UVA. Earlier trials and reviews found little effect of everyday sunscreen use on vitamin D levels,[[cite:neale2019]] but a 2025 randomised trial of daily SPF 50+ use in Australia did find modestly lower levels in the daily-sunscreen group; its authors advise keeping the sunscreen and covering any shortfall through diet or a supplement.[[cite:sund2025]] Daily sunscreen on the face and hands leaves most of the body untouched anyway. The vitamin D article covers the details.

The simplest way to decide on a given day is to look at the number that tracks the relevant radiation. The UVA Index shows live and forecast UVA for your location — including on the cloudy and winter days when the ordinary UV Index looks reassuringly, and misleadingly, low.
