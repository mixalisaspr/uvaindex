How much sunscreen you actually need — and why UVA suffers most

Choosing a sunscreen with real UVA protection is only half the job. The number on the bottle describes performance at a laboratory application density that almost nobody reproduces, and the gap between the two is not a small correction — it is usually the difference between the protection you think you have and a fraction of it. Worse, the shortfall falls hardest on the UVA half of the claim.

The tested density, and the applied density

Every SPF and UVA rating in the world — the FDA's broad-spectrum test, ISO 24443 for the EU's UVA-PF, ISO 24442 for the Japanese PA system — is measured with the product spread at 2 mg per square centimetre of skin. For an average adult body that is about 35 mL, or roughly seven teaspoons, per full application.

Study after study finds real-world application between 0.5 and 1.0 mg/cm² — a quarter to a half of the tested amount. People apply what feels like enough, and a cosmetically pleasant film is a thin one.

The arithmetic is exponential, not proportional

This is the part that surprises people. Halving the amount does not halve the protection — it takes the square root of it. The relationship is approximately:

Read the bottom row carefully. At the density many people genuinely apply, the difference between an SPF 30 and an SPF 50 has almost entirely evaporated — both are delivering somewhere around a factor of two or three. How much you apply matters far more than which number you bought.

Why UVA protection degrades faster

The same exponential penalty applies to UVA protection factors, and then two additional effects compound on top of it — both of which are specific to UVA filters.

First, photostability. Avobenzone (butyl methoxydibenzoylmethane) is the dominant UVA filter available worldwide and it is intrinsically unstable: unprotected, it can lose a large fraction of its absorbing capacity within an hour of sun exposure, as the molecule shifts to a form that no longer absorbs where it is needed. Formulators stabilise it — octocrylene is the classic partner, and modern filters such as bemotrizinol (Tinosorb S) or diethylhexyl 2,6-naphthalate do it better — but stabilisation is a formulation property you cannot read off the front of a bottle. Meanwhile the mineral and next-generation organic UVA filters that are highly photostable are unevenly available: several remain unapproved in the United States, which is covered in UVA labels decoded.

Second, the EU ratio requirement is a ratio. The circled UVA mark certifies that UVA protection is at least one third of the labelled SPF. That is a proportional guarantee, so when under-application collapses your effective SPF from 50 to 7, the UVA protection riding on it collapses in step. Nothing about the mark protects you from the thickness problem.

The consequence: reapplication is not merely about staying topped up for sunburn. It is the main mechanism keeping UVA filters at working strength — which matters because the UVA dose is the one driving photoaging, and it produces no sensation to remind you.

How to actually apply enough

The teaspoon rule

Developed by Cancer Council Australia and the simplest way to hit 2 mg/cm² without weighing anything. One level teaspoon (about 5 mL) for each of:

Body areaAmount
Face, neck and ears1 teaspoon
Each arm1 teaspoon each
Each leg1 teaspoon each
Front of torso1 teaspoon
Back of torso1 teaspoon
Full body total7 teaspoons ≈ 35 mL

A 200 mL bottle therefore contains under six full-body applications. If your holiday bottle lasted a fortnight, you were not applying enough — that arithmetic is a more reliable audit than any impression of how the product felt going on.

The two-finger rule, for face and neck

Squeeze a continuous line of sunscreen along the full length of your index finger and again along your middle finger, from base to tip. That is approximately the right amount for face and neck. It will look like considerably too much. It is not — it is roughly what the laboratory used.

When to reapply

  • Every two hours of continuous outdoor exposure, as a baseline.
  • Immediately after swimming, heavy sweating or towelling, regardless of how long it has been. Towelling is the most effective sunscreen-removal method ever devised.
  • At the stated water-resistance limit. "Water resistant" claims are tested to 40 or 80 minutes of immersion, and that figure is the reapplication instruction, not a promise of all-day performance.
  • Twenty to thirty minutes before going out for the first application, so the film can dry down and form evenly.

The places people miss

Studies using ultraviolet photography — which renders unprotected skin dark and protected skin bright — consistently find around a tenth of the face left uncovered even by people who believe they have applied carefully. The pattern is highly consistent:

  • Eyelids and the inner corners of the eyes, the most-missed region of all, and a common site for basal cell carcinoma. Sunscreen stings there, so people avoid it — which is a strong argument for wraparound UV400 sunglasses covering what sunscreen will not.
  • Ears, especially the upper rim and behind them.
  • Lips, which take no ordinary sunscreen at all; an SPF lip balm is a separate product and needs reapplying more often than anything else.
  • The hairline and any parting, plus thinning or bald areas of the scalp.
  • Back and sides of the neck, and the notch at the base of the throat.
  • Tops of the feet in sandals, and the backs of hands — the hand on top of a steering wheel is a classic asymmetric-damage site.

Four things that do not work

  • Layering products to add SPF. An SPF 15 moisturiser under an SPF 30 sunscreen does not give you 45. In practice you get something close to the higher of the two, and possibly less if the products interfere with each other's films.
  • Relying on SPF in makeup. Foundation and tinted moisturiser are applied at nothing like 2 mg/cm² — you would need many times a normal cosmetic quantity. Treat any SPF in makeup as a small bonus on top of real sunscreen, never as the sunscreen itself.
  • Yesterday's application. Sunscreen is not cumulative or persistent; it lasts as long as the film lasts.
  • An old bottle stored badly. Sunscreens carry an expiry date, typically up to three years, and the stability testing behind it assumes reasonable storage. A bottle that has spent summers in a car — where interior temperatures routinely exceed 60 °C — should be replaced regardless of the printed date. Discard anything separated, grainy or off-smelling.

The realistic conclusion

Almost nobody applies 2 mg/cm² consistently, and telling people to try harder has a poor track record. The productive response is to stop relying on sunscreen alone: clothing and shade deliver their rated protection at whatever thickness they happen to be, never need reapplying, and do not degrade in sunlight. Sunscreen is the right tool for the areas fabric cannot cover — face, neck, hands — where applying it properly is also easiest, because it is the smallest area and the one you can see.

And knowing what you are up against helps. If the UVA Index is 9, a thin smear behaving like SPF 3 is not a plan. If it is 2, it hardly matters.
